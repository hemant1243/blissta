# Make every pixel earn a buyer reason

**On Meta, a direct-response static wins on looks when one picture lands one message in under two seconds, before the viewer's "this is an ad" reflex kicks in.** The picture should be a real moment from the buyer's life or a proof object he can check. Polish, mood, decoration and template sameness lose. Feed ads get roughly 1.7 seconds on mobile, and about three-quarters of "viewable" impressions get no attention at all. Blissta's own tests point the same way. The Corbel receipt with one real object hit **~$8 CPA**. The Ultra Blue fact stack (product plus verifiable facts) runs at **~$26 CPA, with one variation at ~$9**. PainBloc's real caregiver moments were the top spenders, while 27 sibling statics got nothing. The look Hemant approved on Oct 15 is concrete: the product big in the center, short facts on both sides joined to it by thin hairlines, one heavy sans family with the headline about 4x the body, one accent taken from the label on 1–3 things, the price in its own pill, and one textured background that changes with every variant. All **39 design complaints logged Sep 30–Oct 15** share one root cause. The AI starts from a template or a mood instead of the message, never has to justify an element, and never looks at its own render before spending credits. The fix is a fixed order of decisions: lever, ported skeleton, element ledger, pixel spec, a free wireframe, one standalone prompt, one render, then a critique that checks one principle at a time. Small text and fact lists should be set in code. Internal results here are small-sample, and the outside evidence is mostly practitioner anecdote, except the vision-science and eye-tracking findings. Where the two disagree, this report follows Blissta's tested results and says so.

## Feed ads get two seconds, and the picture spends most of them

The attention budget is brutal and well measured. Facebook's own data put mobile time per piece of content at **1.7 seconds**, and people can recall feed content after **0.25 seconds** ([Marketing Dive](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/facebook-why-mobile-video-ads-must-work-fast/446217/); [Facebook for Business](https://www.facebook.com/business/news/updated-features-for-video-ads)). Karen Nelson-Field's camera-based measurement finds that **about 75% of "viewable" inventory gets zero attention**, and that attention "drops sharply after the first second" ([Bizcommunity](https://www.bizcommunity.com/article/karen-nielsen-field-the-attention-deficit-how-to-overcome-it-510470a)). A 2025 peer-reviewed eye-tracking study of a mock Instagram feed measured the "skip the ad" reflex. Sponsored posts drew **about 25% fewer fixations** than organic ones, the content image drew the most fixations of any element, and an early glance at the CTA button or the "Sponsored" label predicted less dwell on the content in **25 of 31 tests** ([Frontiers in Psychology 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12380683/)). NN/g's banner-blindness work agrees: anything whose shape, colored box or embedded text signals "ad" gets ignored ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings/)). So the design job is narrow. Make the dominant picture read as content, put one legible message inside that window, and avoid the visual cues of a banner: a button, a sticker cluster, stacked colored strips.

That same study names the tension Blissta has to manage. The cue viewers cited most often for spotting an ad was **an explicit product in the image (178 mentions)** ([Frontiers 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12380683/)). Its sample was 25-year-olds, though, and Blissta's tested winners all feature "bottles he owns or recognizes," with the product inside the ad's logic ([Design context, Oct 15](</home/user/blissta/research_notes/Winning static ad design system/hemant_project_design_context_oct15.md>)). The resolution: size the product by awareness level. Make it small or part of the scene for cold scroll-stoppers, and big and central when the ad argues from proof or price. Classic print eye-tracking backs a "picture first, headline big" hierarchy. Pieters and Wedel tracked 1,363 print ads. They found the picture captures attention "independent of its size," while attention to text grows with its size, so headline area is the better investment ([University of Michigan](https://news.umich.edu/print-ad-pictures-less-is-more)). Later studies replicated this only partly ([PMC3856423](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3856423)).

The "native beats polished" thesis is popular, but its proof is thin. Smart Marketer reports a native-style image earning a **1.97% "See More" rate versus 0.58%** for the comparison image, on the same copy. The same source warns that engagement wins don't always carry through to CPA ([Smart Marketer](https://smartmarketer.com/2025-facebook-insights-part-2/)). Pattern Brands says the "uglier, more authentic" ad wins three times out of four, but that is self-reported ([Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/marketing/dtc-briefing-why-ugly-ads-are-taking-off/)). One agency notes that polish can win in trust-sensitive categories, where it signals a "real and regulated" company ([Applica](https://applica.agency/blog/why-ugly-ads-win-on-meta-and-why-polished-brand-creative-keeps-losing-in-feed)). For a skeptical 55+ supplement buyer, the defensible middle is **"native but credible"**: a real object or a real place from his life, large clear type, and a product he recognizes. Deliberate crudeness is not the goal. Blissta's own Bible 2.0 reached the same place from its 113-ad scan: "Native beats designed beats polished," with a Sharpie on a foot beating $5K photography ([Design context §4](</home/user/blissta/research_notes/Winning static ad design system/hemant_project_design_context_oct15.md>)).

The audience's eyes set hard limits that no practitioner opinion overrides. WCAG's 4.5:1 contrast minimum was derived from users with 20/40 vision, which it calls typical for people around age 80 ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)). Blue-yellow and pastel confusion affects **about 45% of people in their mid-70s** ([ScienceDaily, Schneck et al.](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/02/140220102614.htm)). In MIT AgeLab glance-reading tests, **condensed type needed 11.2% more time** than regular width ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/glanceable-fonts/)). US health-literacy guidance sets a **16 px floor** for readable text ([ODPHP](https://odphp.health.gov/healthliteracyonline/design-easy-scanning/use-readable-font-thats-least-16-pixels)). NN/g found older users miss elements "among a profusion of other design elements" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-for-senior-citizens/)). Color psychology, by contrast, is weak science. Elliot's review calls the field "nascent," with underpowered studies that overestimate effects ([Frontiers in Psychology 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00368/full)). Luminance contrast is the color evidence that holds up; "blue means trust" is convention. That matters because the feelings-to-color table in Stop the Thumb is one of Blissta's own contradictions.

Either it shows **his exact number or situation in a real object or moment**, or it shows **proof he can read in two seconds** (a scorecard, a receipt, a fact list). It blames the bottle, never him, and it puts the offer next to the product along with a risk reversal ([Design context §10](</home/user/blissta/research_notes/Winning static ad design system/hemant_project_design_context_oct15.md>)).

## The spec in canvas pixels: one family, one accent, one focal point

The pixel numbers come from simple scale math. Both 1080×1080 and 1080×1350 display at roughly 375–430 CSS px wide in the feed, so **on-screen size ≈ canvas px × 0.35–0.40**. Both ratios therefore share the same type floors: the canvases are equally wide, and only height differs. The 16 px health-literacy floor works out to about **44 canvas px**. WCAG's "large text" (about 24 CSS px) works out to about 62–68 canvas px ([ODPHP](https://odphp.health.gov/healthliteracyonline/design-easy-scanning/use-readable-font-thats-least-16-pixels); [W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)). Higgsfield's 1K output and Gemini's 2K output need proportional scaling (×0.95 for 1024 px, ×2 for 2160 px). The type sizes below are a synthesis for a 55+ reader, not a tested standard. The Oct 15 complaint "Why are you making the font so big?" is the reason they come with fit limits.

| Role | 1080×1080 (font-size px) | 1080×1350 (font-size px) | Weight | Fit and spacing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Headline, ≤6 words | 130–160 | 140–170 | Black / ExtraBold | 2–3 lines; line-height 1.05–1.10 |
| Headline, 7–10 words | 100–125 | 110–135 | Black / ExtraBold | 3–4 lines; break lines by meaning |
| Headline, 11–18 words | Stop phrase 104–120; remainder 64–80 | Stop phrase 110–130; remainder 72–88 | Stop phrase heavier or in accent | Stop phrase ≤6 words on lines 1–2; the remainder qualifies |
| Fact / callout | 44–52 | 46–56 | SemiBold / Bold | ≤5 words, ≤2 lines each; gap between facts ≥1× the type size |
| Price pill text | 48–60 | 52–64 | Bold | Pill height ≈1.7× type; side padding ≈0.8× type; one corner radius for the whole ad |
| Risk-reversal line | 40–48 | 44–52 | SemiBold | One line, never inside the pill |
| Must-read floor | 44 | 44 | — | Nothing that has to be read goes below this |
| Non-essential small print | ≥30 | ≥30 | — | Assume a 55+ reader will not read it |
| Hairlines | 3 px | 3 px | — | 2 px vanishes at feed scale; end 12–20 px short of the product edge |
| Margins | 60 px all sides | 64 sides, 72 top, 96 bottom | — | Keep must-read text out of the bottom 96 px of 4:5, where the feed UI can cover it ([Segwise, citing Meta](https://segwise.ai/blog/75-meta-ad-ideas-creative-testing)) |

A heavy geometric sans averages about 0.6 em per character, so the usable 952 px line holds roughly **10 characters at 160 px, 13 at 120, 16 at 100, 21 at 76 and 33 at 48**. Check this capacity before choosing a size. A worked example is the current MB winner, whose headline runs 75 characters. On 1080×1080, "14mg of real / methylene blue" fits at 110 px on two lines (~240 px tall). "and 11 more reasons to try it for fibro fatigue" fits at 64 px on two lines (~150 px). The headline block then uses about 37% of the height, leaving ~430 px for the pouch. On 1080×1350 the same block uses about 30%, and the pouch can reach ~600 px. The ratio of stop phrase to fact text then lands at about 2.5x, which is Stop the Thumb's floor. A short headline can reach the approved ~4x. **The rule is to measure the hierarchy on the stop phrase, not on the whole headline.** The font itself is unspecified anywhere in Blissta's docs, which say "no logo, color or font guide exists" ([internal evidence Q2](</home/user/blissta/research_notes/Winning static ad design system/blissta_internal_design_evidence.md>)). Pick one licensed, normal-width heavy geometric sans and lock it. Montserrat at 800 for headlines and 600 for facts is a free default that fits the approved look. Use the same file for code-set type and for describing the font in prompts.

Color follows the function chain from rule 4, applied per product. Sample the label to get the product's hue and lightness. Choose a ground whose *lightness* is clearly opposite, made of a real material from the buyer's world. Set text at maximum contrast: near-black #111111 on light grounds, white or warm off-white on dark scenes ([DS v2.1](https://newworkspace-rhk2011.slack.com/docs/T0963M61FV3/F0C8KLY0W1E)). Every text block must reach at least 4.5:1 against the pixels directly behind it, and 7:1 where possible. Put the label accent on at most three named items. Set the light temperature by message: cold for the problem state, warm for the answer. The whole ad uses **four colors at most**: ground, text, accent and the product itself. Avoid yellow on white, blue on green, and pastel pairs that carry meaning (for example "before" vs "after"), because 55+ eyes confuse them ([ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/02/140220102614.htm)). Separate states by lightness, not hue alone.

| Product | Packaging | Grounds that separate | Grounds banned | Accent (1–3 items) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MB Ultra Blue gummies | Navy/blue pouch | Warm off-white linen, cream plaster, light oak, warm stone, a morning kitchen counter | Cobalt, navy or any blue ground (the "forcing the color" rejection); flat beige "luxury" | Label navy: the price pill, one highlighted phrase, at most one more |
| Corbel / Corvael nattokinase | Navy glass, black cap, gold wordmark, cream panel | Light warm surfaces. A dark slate scene (as in [CORBEL-30](https://newworkspace-rhk2011.slack.com/docs/T0963M61FV3/F0C7XATHPV5)) only with a rim light that separates the navy glass | Navy ("navy on navy = invisible"); the one cream template on every ad; oxblood | Label gold *or* navy, one per ad |
| PainBloc PEA | Per the saved reference element (the notes record no hex) | Sample the label and go opposite in lightness | The bottle's own hue family; the flat saturated navy "no scene" fill used in the Oct 12 canvas ([PEA-45](https://newworkspace-rhk2011.slack.com/docs/T0963M61FV3/F0C8AAFF51R)) | Sampled label color |

Hierarchy needs exactly one dominant thing. In proof and choice ads that is the product. In belief and scroll ads it is the moment or the artifact. It should carry two to three times the visual weight of the next element, and each added element dilutes the others ([PMC3856423](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3856423)). Write the eye path before placing anything (Stop the Thumb decision 7), typically stop phrase → picture/product → proof → offer. Cap the visible elements at about five: headline, picture or product, one proof layer, one offer object, and the ground. Two tests confirm the hierarchy. The **grayscale test**: desaturate the ad, and the order must hold. The **35% test**: shrink the ad to feed size (about 380 px wide), and the stop phrase and focal object must still read.

Imagery rules come straight from the wins and the rejections. Use a real moment from his life or a proof object, never a metaphor, a decorative anatomy diagram, or a "random AI couple in a kitchen." Allow one prop at most, and only one that has a buyer reason. Use the real product from the saved reference element, with the label unchanged. When small label print garbles, paste the real product photo over it, as Mark already does. Never make him look "old, sad or broken" ([static SOP notes](/home/user/blissta/brand/drive/static-ads-and-sops.md)). Budget extra rerolls for AI's known weak spots: aged hands, older faces, legible handwriting, exact counts and small text ([handbook §4.6](/home/user/blissta/bot/knowledge/OPERATOR_HANDBOOK.md)). For text on the image, place approved copy word for word and never add, cut or "improve" it. No eyebrow tags ("For men 50+ who…") unless the copy contains them. No em dashes, no country or state, no button, one highlighted phrase at most, and line breaks that follow meaning.

## Why the AI keeps failing, and the nine-pass workflow that stops it

The design context names the pattern behind all 39 complaints: "the AI starts from a template or a mood, not from the message, and nothing forces it to justify each element before it renders." Three mechanisms make that default sticky. First, language models collapse toward the *typical* answer because human preference data rewards familiarity. Asking the model to list several options with probabilities raises output diversity **1.6–2.1×** ([Verbalized Sampling, arXiv 2510.01171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01171v3)). Second, a prompt written top to bottom tends to describe the ad as stacked rows, and image models render zone words literally. Third, a prompt-writer that never sees its render is "blind to the rendered visual outcome" ([VFLM, arXiv 2603.22187](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22187)). Asking a vision model for one holistic score doesn't fix that, because GPT-4o-class critics "do not separate individual principles well" ([PRISM, arXiv 2606.00592](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00592)).

| Complaint (count) | Why the AI does it | Process fix |
|---|---|---|
| Same template or skeleton (8) | Typicality bias. The previous prompt in the same chat anchors the next. Archetype lists get treated as a menu of defaults. "Same as V1" shortcuts | A batch skeleton registry: each concept claims a different ported skeleton and a different scene before any prompt is written. Run v2.4's swap test (if you can swap the words between two concepts and nothing looks wrong, they're one concept). Every prompt is written fresh, with no copy-paste |
| Stacked bands, forced zones (5) | Prompt grammar lists regions ("top 25% plain wall"), and the model draws them as strips. The template's "CTA + bottom bar" block literally asks for a band | Describe **one continuous photograph with one light source**, and place type on a named scene surface ("on the plaster wall above the pouch"). Ban zone vocabulary (list below). For designed grounds, render a text-free plate and set type in code |
| Forced or wrong color (6) | The model reasons "brand color = product color" and matches the ground to the pouch. Feelings tables. "Luxury" beige | The color function chain. The ground must sit outside the product's hue family and be opposite in lightness. One label accent on ≤3 named items. Grayscale and 35% tests |
| Forced visuals, props, metaphors (5) | The "visual" slot gets filled with generic symbols when no real moment has been named | Visual-or-none with a removal test. The picture must name the real moment (from VOC) or the proof object. One prop at most, with its buyer reason written down |
| Type and hierarchy (4) | Pixel sizes are guessed, then found out by re-rendering at 1.5 credits each. The model jams list items together | A fixed type scale, a free wireframe, and code-set type for lists, so "font too big" is caught before a credit is spent |
| Design with no buyer reason (3) | Nothing requires justification | The element ledger (below). An element with no buyer reason is deleted |
| Ignored references, invented layouts (3) | Inventing is cheaper than reading the breakdowns | A port step: name the library reference, answer the six breakdown questions, and state what is borrowed (layout and light only). Never rebuild a winner without its image |
| Touched approved copy (3) | The model "helps" by editing to make a crowded render fit | Copy is locked and checked verbatim by OCR. If it doesn't fit, change the skeleton or size (above the floor). Otherwise stop and ask |
| Eyebrow or callout tags (2) | A kicker label is a template default | Banned unless the locked copy contains it |

### The nine passes, in order

**Pass 1, intake:** lock the approved copy, the product, the facts that will appear (from §13 only), the awareness level, the lever and the message type. **Pass 2, strategy card:** one sentence each for who, moment and feeling (Stop the Thumb decision 1), the single stopper, and the eye path 1–5. **Pass 3, port:** pick the library reference (the 70 breakdowns, Blissta's winners) that fits the lever and message. Answer the Designer Brief's six questions (concept, eye path, offer device, headline job, scroll stopper, what stays), and write down what is borrowed ([static SOP notes](/home/user/blissta/brand/drive/static-ads-and-sops.md)). Diversity enters here, in the choice of reference, without inventing anything. Verbalized sampling fits: "list five library formats that could carry this message, with the probability you would default to each; drop any skeleton already claimed in this batch." This reconciles the anti-sameness research with Hemant's "we steal proven formats, we don't invent." **Pass 4, element ledger.** **Pass 5, spec:** skeleton zones in px for the target ratio, type sizes from the scale, hexes from the color chain, and the type route. **Pass 6, free wireframe:** Claude draws the layout in Python at full canvas size. Gray boxes stand in for product and scene, and the real copy is set at the spec sizes in the locked font. Claude views it at 100% and at 35%, and fixes fit and hierarchy here, at zero credits. This answers "Why are you wasting my credit?" directly, and the wireframe becomes the final type layer when the route is code. **Pass 7, prompt:** one standalone prompt with the block structure below. **Pass 8, render one:** a single render (Nano Banana 2 1K in Higgsfield for Claude's draft, or Nano Banana Pro 2K for Mark), viewed by the critic. **Pass 9, critique, fix and human OK:** the one-principle checks in the next section, targeted fixes only, at most two rerolls per image, then Hemant's OK before any variants ([Ad Factory SOP](/home/user/blissta/brand/SOP_AD_FACTORY.md)).

### The element ledger is the buyer-reason gate

Before any prompt, every element that will appear gets one row. Each row names the buyer question it answers, the layer it belongs to (hook, picture, proof or offer), and what is lost if it is removed. An element whose removal loses nothing is cut. Only one element per layer may play each role, which is Bible Part 1's stacking rule. Here is the ledger for the approved Ultra Blue fact stack:

| Element | Buyer reason | Layer | If removed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Headline (locked copy) | Names his condition and a dose he can verify | Hook | No message |
| Pouch, ~45% of height, centered | He buys bottles he recognizes, and the label is where he checks the 14mg | Picture | Nothing to verify or buy |
| 4–6 short facts with hairlines | Each is a claim he can check on the label. The hairline shows it belongs to this product | Proof | Loses the two-second proof |
| Navy price pill | Answers "what does it cost?" for warm traffic | Offer | Fine on cold traffic, so drop it there |
| Linen-textured warm ground | Separates the navy pouch by lightness and avoids a dead fill | Ground | The pouch disappears |
| ~~Numbered circles on facts~~ | None. Decoration posing as structure, and #1 repeated the headline | — | Nothing lost: delete |
| ~~Red "71% off" sticker on the bottle~~ | Duplicates the pill and fights the label color | — | Nothing lost: delete |

### When to set type in code

"AI makes the picture, code sets the type" is already allowed in v2.4 rule 14, but it is barely used. Practitioners and vendors recommend text-free generation plus a separate type layer for exact spelling, exact sizes and pixel-identical headline variants ([Alex Cooper](https://alexcooper.beehiiv.com/p/how-to-make-static-ads-with-chatgpt-4o-plus-some-freebies); [Goodeye, via AI workflow note](</home/user/blissta/research_notes/Winning static ad design system/ai_design_workflow.md>)). Image models treat label text as texture and repaint it ([Snappyit](https://snappyit.ai/blog/why-ai-gets-text-wrong-on-products)). The tradeoff is real, though. Nano Banana 2 sets fully locked headline lines correctly, and code type on a photographic scene can look pasted on. No source compares the CTR of in-model and overlaid text. So the decision is made per element:

| Set it in code (Python/PIL or HTML → screenshot) | Let the image model render it | Composite the real photo |
|---|---|---|
| Any fact list or reasons list with ≥3 items (S1, S6) | A headline on a scene surface, every line locked in quotes, ≤12 words | Any product label whose small print garbles |
| Any number, price, mg value or percentage below ~100 px (the "2,7700mg" failure) | Lettering that belongs to an object: Sharpie on skin, receipt print, warning tape, handwriting (S4, S3 titles) | Competitor bottles, once Hemant clears their use |
| Hook tests on one approved plate, so only the copy changes | — | — |
| Any text that failed one reroll | — | — |
| Everything on a plain textured ground (S1, S5), where code type is native to the design | — | — |

For Mark's Gemini 2K route, Claude delivers the type layer as a transparent PNG at 2160 px wide (every px value in this report ×2), which he places over the text-free plate. When the route is code, the prompt has to *reserve* the type area as part of the scene ("the upper plaster wall stays smooth and evenly lit"). This is the "text-safe zone contract" the hybrid workflow depends on ([AI workflow note §2](</home/user/blissta/research_notes/Winning static ad design system/ai_design_workflow.md>)).

### The standalone prompt structure

The block structure merges Google's "subject + action + location + composition + style" formula with OpenAI's advice to index references by role and repeat the preserve list on every edit pass ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/ultimate-prompting-guide-for-nano-banana); [OpenAI Cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/multimodal/image-gen-models-prompting-guide)). It describes a photograph, not a layout.

```text
REFERENCES  Image 1 = [product] packshot: copy its shape, colors, cap and every label word exactly; do not redraw label text.
            Image 2 (optional) = [library winner]: borrow composition and light direction only.
CANVAS      [1:1 | 4:5], one continuous full-bleed photograph, edge to edge.
SCENE       [his real place + the one surface the product stands on + at most one prop, named].
LIGHT/LENS  One [window/lamp] light from [direction], [cool 5600K problem | warm 3200K answer], [lens, depth of field], real contact shadow.
PRODUCT     [Image 1 product] standing on [surface], [x]% of frame height, [position relative to scene objects], label facing camera.
TYPE        Route A: Render only the text in quotes, spelled exactly, in a heavy geometric sans-serif, sentence case, color #[hex]:
              line 1 "[...]" line 2 "[...]" (cap height ≈ [n]% of frame height), left-aligned on [named surface].
            Route B: The [named surface] stays smooth and evenly lit; the photograph contains no lettering anywhere.
COLOR       Ground material [linen/plaster/oak] #[hex]; text #[hex]; accent #[label hex] appears only on [item(s)].
KEEP        Only the quoted text appears; Image 1 label unchanged; one light direction; one continuous surface.
```

Strike these words from every prompt, because the models render them literally or they invite the rejected looks: **band, strip, bar, panel, section, zone, header, footer, "top N% plain," poster, badge, chip, sticker, eyebrow, luxury, premium feel**. The same goes for any emotion word standing in for a color ("trustworthy blue"). Phrase exclusions positively where possible ("only the quoted text appears"), as Google's guide recommends. Every prompt stands alone, because Mark receives prompts in a Slack canvas and "same as V1" was a counted failure.

## Critique one principle at a time, on the rendered image

The research is consistent on one point: critique works when the critic *sees the render* and checks explicit principles one at a time, preferably pointing at the region at fault. Duan et al.'s guideline-plus-few-shot visual critique closed half the gap to human experts ([arXiv 2412.16829](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16829)). Render-look-refine loops beat single-pass generation for layouts and front-end code ([arXiv 2604.05839](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05839v1)). PRISM showed that general vision models miss targeted violations when asked to judge holistically ([arXiv 2606.00592](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00592)). No study has tested this loop on direct-response statics against CPA, so the checklist below is engineered from that research and from Blissta's complaint log, not validated against sales. Each row is a separate pass with its own short prompt. The critic sees the PNG before it sees the prompt, so it judges what is there rather than what was intended. It is shown the approved jobs (c2b4af39, d3e5d723, c2d7ff20, 0176e83f, 6138b3cf) and the rejected Oct 15 renders as anchors. It returns pass or fail, the location, and one fix.

| # | Principle (one per pass) | How to check | Pass condition | Guards against |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Copy fidelity | OCR (code) vs the locked copy; every number vs the §13 facts | Verbatim; every number is live | Touched copy, old facts, garbled small print |
| 2 | Two-second message | View at 35% (≈380 px wide) for two seconds | You can state who, the moment and the feeling | "Will someone read this shit ad?" |
| 3 | One focal point | 35% plus blur | Exactly one dominant object or moment | Two focal points, clutter |
| 4 | Hierarchy ratio | Measure cap heights | Stop phrase ≥2.5× fact text (target 3–4×); product is the largest object | Weak type, no hierarchy |
| 5 | Size floors and fit | Measure | No must-read text <44 px; headline within the scale; nothing within the margins | "Font so big," wasted credits |
| 6 | Grouping and spacing | Inspect lists | Gap between facts ≥1× type size; one left edge per column; facts tied by a hairline, not numbers | List jammed together, meaningless numbering |
| 7 | Type system | Inspect | One family, ≤2 weights, sentence case; caps only for a ≤5-word punch or object print; no condensed face | All-caps headlines, mixed fonts |
| 8 | Color budget | Count hues; grayscale | ≤4 colors; accent on ≤3 named items; ground outside the product's hue family; order holds in grayscale | Forced color, two-color CTA, red-sticker clash |
| 9 | Contrast | Sample pixels behind each text block (code) | ≥4.5:1 everywhere, ≥7:1 for text under 64 px | Muted "luxury" invisibility |
| 10 | Continuous scene | Inspect edges and light | No bands, strips, panels, seams or glued photos; one light direction | Stacked bands, two photos glued |
| 11 | Product integrity and placement | Compare against Image 1 | Label intact; size per lever; inside the logic (end of the hairlines, the answer side, in the scene); nothing stuck on it | Bottle in the corner, grey pouch, sticker on the product |
| 12 | Ledger reconciliation | List every visible element | Each one appears in the ledger with a buyer reason; extras are removed | Decoration without a buyer reason, eyebrows, props |
| 13 | Offer discipline | Inspect | One offer object (+ ≤1 risk-reversal line); none on cold traffic; no button | Sticker + pill + proof line stacks |
| 14 | Native register | Squint: post or banner? | Reads as a photo or object from his life, not a template | "Looks like AI slop / a template" |
| 15 | Compliance | Checklist | No em dashes, country, cure/treat/replace claims, fake reviews or "talk to your doctor" | Rejections, account risk |
| 16 | Batch distinctness (batch level) | Thumbnail grid of the batch, side by side | Swap test passes between concepts; hook triplets pass the series test (related, not identical) | Same template across concepts |

The loop has three hard limits. **Measurable checks run in code, not in a model**: OCR match, contrast ratio, type sizes, and overlap between text boxes and the product box. **Only failed principles get fixed**, with the preserve list restated in each edit prompt. **At most two rerolls per image.** After that, change the route (code type, composite label) or drop the concept, because open-ended agent loops "fall into infinite loops or premature convergence" and the Ad Factory SOP already caps rerolls at two ([AI workflow note §4](</home/user/blissta/research_notes/Winning static ad design system/ai_design_workflow.md>); [Ad Factory SOP](/home/user/blissta/brand/SOP_AD_FACTORY.md)). The first render of each concept still goes to Hemant before any variants, and Mark receives only prompts that have passed checks 1–13 on Claude's own render.

## Conclusion

The bottleneck is the order in which decisions get made, not taste. Almost every counted complaint can be prevented *before* a credit is spent. Choose the lever and a ported skeleton, justify every element in a ledger, set exact pixel sizes in a free wireframe, and describe one continuous photograph instead of a stack of zones. Once that order is fixed, most of the internal contradictions dissolve. They were never real disagreements about what wins. They were rules written for different levers or stages and applied to everything. A plain ground is right for a fact stack and wrong for a belief ad. Locked palettes suit a scaling winner and smother an exploration batch. Ten words is right for a scroll-stopper and too tight for a proof stack. Setting fact lists, prices and hook tests in code turns the vaguest part of the brief into a spec that can be checked, and it makes headline tests clean, because only the copy changes.

The open questions should be settled by Blissta's own spend, not by more outside reading. Three tests matter most: phone-UI natives versus physical-object natives for MB and PainBloc, 4:5 versus 1:1 on the same concept, and in-model versus code-set headlines on the same plate. Until those numbers exist, the best guide is the evidence Blissta has already paid for: proof structure beats mood, a real moment beats a metaphor, and the product belongs big and inside the argument.
