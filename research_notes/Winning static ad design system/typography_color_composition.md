# Typography, Color and Composition for Direct-Response Mobile Static Ads (Health/Supplement, Adults 55+), plus Transferable Principles from Classic Design Masters

Scope note: Research done 2026-10-15. The evidence base is uneven. The strongest evidence covers vision science (aging eyes, contrast) and accessibility standards (WCAG, federal health-literacy guides). There is moderate, partly contested evidence on print typography (Wheildon, Ogilvy, Pieters & Wedel eye-tracking). Color psychology evidence is weak or early-stage. Most "DTC static ad font" advice online is practitioner opinion, and I found no rigorous study of statics in the Meta feed. Each section separates evidence from convention.

---

## 1. Typography for mobile feed statics: sizes, weights, case, serif vs sans, fonts, spacing, word counts, text treatments

### Takeaway
- **Size.** On a 1080x1350 (4:5) canvas shown about 390–430 CSS px wide, the canvas is scaled to roughly 36–40%. Text therefore needs to be about 2.7x its intended on-screen pixel size.
- **Minimums for a 55+ audience:**
  - Supporting text: at least ~44 px on canvas, which is about 16 CSS px on screen.
  - Preferred supporting text: ~56–64 px on canvas, about 20–24 CSS px.
  - Headlines: at least ~80–90 px on canvas, preferably 100–160 px.
- **Words.** Keep headlines short, since fewer words let the type run larger.
- **Case and weight.** Use sentence or title case rather than long all-caps runs. Use bold or semibold weights and avoid thin ones.
- **Fonts.** Use no more than two typefaces.
- **Reversed type.** Light-on-dark text should be limited to short headline-size lines. Do not use it for paragraphs.

### Cited Findings
**Size baselines from standards and research:**
- US federal health-literacy guidance (ODPHP *Health Literacy Online*, Section 5.3) says to "Use a readable font that's at least 16 pixels" for web health content — [ODPHP](https://odphp.health.gov/healthliteracyonline/design-easy-scanning/use-readable-font-thats-least-16-pixels)
- WCAG 2.2 defines "large text" as 18 pt regular or 14 pt bold, which it equates to about 24 px and 18.5 px. Large text may use a lower 3:1 contrast ratio, while smaller text needs 4.5:1 — [W3C WCAG 2.2 Understanding 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)
- A 2022 systematic review of font size for older adults on mobile devices reported a range of findings:
  - Recommended sizes on tablets were about 14–22 pt for English text.
  - Chatrangsan & Petrie (2019) found 18 pt was read faster than 14 or 16 pt on iPad.
  - Yeh (2015) recommended 14 pt (42 arcmin) for reading, and Yeh (2020) recommended 22 pt (66 arcmin) for button labels.
  - Hou et al. (2020) on iPhone 6 found 14 px best for information search and 17–20 px for intensive reading.
  - The review states that "older adults prefer larger font size irrespective of device display resolution, device type or font typeface."
  - Reading speed drops sharply below a critical print size, and "bigger may not always mean better."
  - The evidence base is small: 12 papers, some with only 12 participants, using inconsistent metrics.
  — [PMC9376262 systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9376262/)
- The same review gives a rule of thumb for a 40 cm viewing distance: visual angle in degrees ≈ point size ÷ 20. It also cites Hasegawa et al. (2006), who found that people hold the screen closer as they age and as text gets smaller — [PMC9376262](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9376262/)
- A study of older adults (mean age 70) found they rated 14 pt as more legible than 12 pt and significantly preferred larger 14 pt sans-serif fonts — [ERAU portfolio summary](https://portfolio.erau.edu/en/publications/examining-perceptions-of-online-text-size-and-typeface-legibility/) (seen as a search snippet, not read in full)
- Print guides for older readers adapted from NIA guidance set a minimum of 12 pt and prefer 14 pt. One secondary summary reports that NIA advises at least 14 pt for printed materials — [Mass.gov accessible print materials](https://www.mass.gov/doc/accessible-print-materials/download); [DC Age-Friendly handout](https://agefriendly.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/agefriendly/publication/attachments/Writing%20Handout%20One%20Page.pdf); [CDC Simply Put](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/11938/cdc_11938_DS1.pdf). The NIA page itself returned HTTP 405 and could not be read directly.

**Serif vs sans:**
- The evidence is mixed. In Chatrangsan & Petrie (2019), UK older readers found sans serif easier and less tiring, but comprehension was better with serif and larger fonts. Typeface did not affect reading time — [PMC9376262](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9376262/)
- The guidance on serif vs sans depends on the medium:
  - NIA's web checklist, as summarized, recommends sans serif on screens and says to "Avoid the use of serif, novelty, and display typefaces."
  - Print guides (Mass.gov, ATSDR) prefer serifs such as Times New Roman for body text.
  - Sources: [search summary of NIA-adapted guides](https://www.mass.gov/doc/accessible-print-materials/download); [ATSDR toolkit](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/communications-toolkit/documents/13_Guide-to-Materials-Development-final-01112016_508.pdf)
- Colin Wheildon's 1986 tests for *Type & Layout* are a print study of body copy. Good comprehension was 67% for serif body text versus 12% for sans-serif — [Cutting Edge PR](https://cuttingedgepr.com/articles/serif-typefaces-best-for-body-copy-of-printed-publications/); [SOFII](https://sofii.org/article/sorry-but-youre-just-not-my-type)
- Wheildon's tests have been criticized. A Microsoft-affiliated typography researcher noted that the serif and sans faces compared "do not otherwise have much in common" — [John D. Berry](https://johndberry.com/category/typography/page/5)
- Wheildon reportedly considered sans acceptable for short text such as headlines and call-outs — [SOFII](https://sofii.org/article/sorry-but-youre-just-not-my-type)

**Ogilvy's typography rules (convention backed by his and Starch-era readership data):**
- "Serif typestyles are the easiest to read."
- "Words with all capital letters are also hard to read," so use upper and lower case.
- Reverse type is "almost impossible to read."
- A subhead between headline and body increases readership.
- A drop-initial raises readership about 13%.
- Space between paragraphs raises readership about 12%.
- Source: [ProfitAdvisors summary of Ogilvy](https://www.profitadvisors.com/ogilvy.shtml)

**Reverse type (light on dark):**
- Wheildon reports that serif body text in black on white was comprehended well by 70% of readers. In white on black it was 0% good, 12% fair and 88% poor — [Cutting Edge PR](https://cuttingedgepr.com/using-reverse-type-dramatically-reduces-reader-comprehension/)
- Treat this as a long-copy print finding. It is not evidence about a three-word headline on a phone.

**Number of fonts and headline length:**
- Google's display-ad guidance:
  - "Keep headlines short... The shorter the headline, the larger the text can be."
  - "Use one or, at most, two fonts."
  - Avoid mixing sizes, kerning or justification inside a headline.
  - Source: [Google Rich Media dynamic creative best practices](https://support.google.com/richmedia/answer/2852705?hl=en)
- Practitioner guidance avoids Thin and ExtraLight weights at small sizes on mobile. It recommends a solid backing plate or box behind text that sits on busy photos and warns against near-white on white or near-black on dark — [DesignYourWay](https://www.designyourway.net/blog/best-fonts-for-facebook-ads/) (blog-quality source)

**Amount of text in the image:**
- Meta dropped its enforced 20% image-text rule in September 2020. Its guidance still says: "Avoid too much image text. We've found that images with less than 20% text perform better." — [Adzooma](https://adzooma.com/blog/facebook-advertising-removes-20-text-rule-on-images); [Social News Desk](https://www.socialnewsdesk.com/blog/facebooks-20-rule-is-no-more/)
- Meta's own primary page was not reachable, so this relies on consistent quotes in trade blogs.

**Bold and recall:**
- Price et al. (2016), cited in the review, found bold text was recalled better than regular or italic text. This is a memory effect, not legibility — [PMC9376262](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9376262/)

### Inferences
- **Feed-scale conversion (my calculation).**
  - Feed images usually render at about 375–430 CSS px wide (typical phone viewport widths), so the scale factor is about 0.35–0.40. Canvas px ≈ desired CSS px × 2.7.
  - Smallest legible supporting text (16 CSS px, the ODPHP floor) ≈ 43–46 px on a 1080-wide canvas.
  - For 55+ readers, aim for supporting text equal to WCAG "large text" (about 24 CSS px, or 18.5 px bold) ≈ 50–65 px on canvas.
  - These px sizes are font-size. Many display faces have small x-heights, so check cap and x-height visually rather than trusting the number.
  - Legal disclaimers and footnotes at about 30–36 px on canvas (11–13 CSS px) will be effectively unreadable to many people over 55. That is acceptable only for text that does not need to be read.
- **Recommended type scale for 1080x1350 aimed at 55+ (synthesis, not a tested standard):**
  - Headline: 100–160 px, bold or black, about 3–8 words over 2–3 lines.
  - Subhead: 56–72 px, semibold.
  - Proof or badge text: 44–56 px bold.
  - CTA: 48–60 px bold on a button bar.
  - Line spacing: 1.05–1.15 for display headlines and about 1.3–1.5 for any multi-line supporting text.
  - Line length: no more than about 25–30 characters per line at headline size, which is forced by the 1080 width anyway.
- **All caps.** Ogilvy and the senior-print guides agree on avoiding long all-caps runs. Short all-caps labels such as "NEW", "DOCTOR-FORMULATED" or a 2–4 word kicker are fine and are standard DR practice. Set headlines of 5 or more words in sentence or title case for 55+ readers.
- **Condensed faces** (Impact, Anton, Bebas Neue, Oswald, League Gothic) fit more characters at a larger size, which is why DR uses them. Condensed letterforms have tighter counters and less distinct shapes, so for 55+ readers prefer a bold normal-width face, or a semi-condensed one, at a slightly smaller size. Avoid ultra-condensed faces for anything beyond 2–4 words. This is a reasoned inference; I found no direct study.
- **Serif or sans, by format:**
  - Native-looking "advertorial" or "news" statics: a newspaper serif (Georgia, Times, Merriweather, Source Serif) supports the editorial frame and suits Wheildon's and Ogilvy's print findings for longer text.
  - Bold-claim statics: a sturdy sans (Inter, Helvetica/Arial, Roboto, Montserrat, Proxima-style) is safest on screens.
  - Handwritten fonts (sticky-note or "doctor's note" formats) should be limited to a few words, since script faces reduce legibility. Use clear casual scripts rather than connected cursive.
- **Text treatments.**
  - On photos, a solid or semi-opaque backing bar or box is more reliable than a drop shadow.
  - A soft shadow helps light type only slightly.
  - Thin outlines (stroke) on type tend to look cheap and add visual noise at feed scale. I found no study on this; it is convention.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed or platform-published study measuring legibility or CTR by font size on Meta feed statics, and no Meta-published minimum font size for image text.
- I found no reliable source documenting which typefaces DTC or DR brands use most often for statics. The font names above (Anton, Bebas, Impact, Inter, Georgia) reflect practitioner convention, not sourced data. A swipe-file audit (for example via Trendtrack or the Meta Ad Library) would be needed to quantify usage.
- Wheildon's all-caps headline findings could not be verified. Search results did not cover them.
- No source in this session gave line-spacing (leading) numbers specifically for older readers. The 1.3–1.5 figure is standard web typography convention and is not sourced here.

## 2. Older adults' vision: presbyopia, contrast sensitivity, color perception, glare, clutter

### Takeaway
Aging reduces acuity, contrast sensitivity and blue-yellow color discrimination, and increases glare from lens scatter. For 55+ viewers, design for high luminance contrast (at least 4.5:1, ideally 7:1 for small text). Do not rely on blue-vs-green, yellow-vs-white or pastel distinctions. Keep layouts uncluttered with large type.

### Cited Findings
**Contrast standards and their link to aging:**
- WCAG's 4.5:1 minimum is derived from users with 20/40 acuity: 3:1 × 1.5 contrast-sensitivity loss = 4.5:1.
- The AAA level of 7:1 matches 20/80 acuity.
- WCAG states "20/40 is commonly reported as typical visual acuity of elders at roughly age 80" and cites "the loss of contrast sensitivity that typically accompanies aging."
- Source: [W3C WCAG 2.2 Understanding 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)

**Color vision changes:**
- In a study of 865 older adults, color-vision abnormalities were uncommon before 70. About 45% of people in their mid-70s and up to 50% of those 85 and older had them. Nearly 80% of abnormalities involved confusing pastel blue vs purple and yellow vs green or yellow-green — [ScienceDaily, Schneck et al. 2014](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/02/140220102614.htm)
- Yellowing of the lens is considered the main cause of reduced short-wavelength (blue) sensitivity. Blue-yellow confusion is estimated to rise about 1.6% per year with age, versus about 1% for red-green — [Frontiers in Integrative Neuroscience 2023 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/integrative-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnint.2023.1129315/pdf)
- A guide adapted from NIA guidance advises avoiding yellow, blue and green in close proximity because older readers find them hard to tell apart — [Michael J. Fox Foundation guide](https://www.michaelJfox.org/sites/default/files/media/document/C5_GuideCreatingMaterialsforOlderVolunteers.pdf) (content seen via search snippet; the PDF text could not be extracted)

**Glare and contrast sensitivity:**
- The aging lens "becomes less clear and... begins to scatter more light," which "reduces the contrast of the retinal image" — [Lighting Research Center (RPI/AARP)](https://www.lrc.rpi.edu/programs/lightHealth/AARP/healthcare/lightingOlderAdults/agingEye.asp)
- Contrast sensitivity declines with age, with and without glare — [Turkish J. Ophthalmology](https://oftalmoloji.org/articles/the-comparison-of-scotopic-and-mesopic-contrast-sensitivity-with-glare-between-pseudophakic-and-phakic-eyes/2872)
- On mobile, glare did not significantly reduce readability, but participants changed their viewing distance to cope (Ko et al. 2014). Higher display contrast improved readability (Fujikake et al. 2007) — [PMC9376262](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9376262/)

**Print guidance:**
- Use dark lettering on a light background, on non-glossy paper. Off-white or light-yellow matte paper is suggested — [Mass.gov](https://www.mass.gov/doc/accessible-print-materials/download)
- Use lots of white space — [CDC Simply Put](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/11938/cdc_11938_DS1.pdf)

**Usability evidence (NN/g):**
- NN/g has run three rounds of research with 123 users aged 65+ (2001, 2013, 2018–19), reported in [NN/g, Kane 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-for-senior-citizens/). It found:
  - Vision and dexterity decline with age.
  - Web-use ability declines about 0.8% per year from 25 to 60.
  - Mobile text was often "too small and lightly colored."
  - Error messages were missed "among a profusion of other design elements."
- Secondary sources report older NN/g figures: users 65+ were 43% slower than those aged 21–55, and task success was 55.3% vs 74.5% — [MedCity News (sponsored)](https://medcitynews.com/2013/06/the-case-for-making-your-website-senior-friendly/). These are older and secondhand; verify them before quoting.
- In a content analysis of 100 senior resource websites, color and contrast had the largest effect on overall accessibility, ahead of font size and organization — [PMC7741326](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7741326/)

### Inferences
**Rules for 55+ statics:**
1. Text contrast should be at least 4.5:1 for all supporting text and 7:1 where possible. Headlines at 24+ CSS px equivalent can go down to 3:1, but aim higher.
2. Never set light gray on white, or mid-gray on black.
3. Avoid yellow text on white and white text on yellow, and avoid pastel-blue/lavender or yellow/green pairings that carry meaning, such as "before" vs "after" colors.
4. Signal differences with luminance (light vs dark) as well as hue. For example, a dark-navy "after" panel against a pale "before" panel.
5. Keep competing elements to about 3–5 per ad (headline, visual, one proof element, CTA, brand). Clutter is the main failure mode NN/g observed.

**Glare.** On phones the "glossy paper" issue becomes screen glare and outdoor use. High-contrast, heavy type survives glare better than thin or low-contrast type. Large dark backgrounds can show reflections, but this is not a reason to avoid dark ads.

### Gaps
- I could not read the NIA's own "Making Your Printed Health Materials Older Adult Friendly" page (HTTP 405), and the PDF of the NIA-adapted guide could not be text-extracted. NIA's points are cited from secondary adaptations.
- I found no AARP-published design guideline in this session.
- No source quantified how much the presbyopia share among 55–65-year-olds changes optimal mobile ad type size. The general fact that near vision needs correction from the mid-40s is background knowledge, consistent with NN/g's note that "people in their 40s already have reduced eyesight."

## 3. Color: evidence vs myth; backgrounds; feed contrast (light and dark mode); emotion pairings

### Takeaway
Rigorous evidence for specific color-to-emotion effects is thin. The defensible claims are narrow:
- Color meaning depends on context (color-in-context theory).
- Red has some evidence for signaling danger and avoidance in achievement contexts.
- A color that fits the brand personality improves liking and purchase intent (Labrecque & Milne).

Most "blue = trust, green = health" claims are associative conventions rather than proven causal effects. For feed performance, contrast against the Facebook/Instagram UI and legibility matter more than hue symbolism.

### Cited Findings
**What the color-psychology literature actually shows:**
- Elliot's 2015 review in *Frontiers in Psychology* says the literature is at a "nascent stage of development." It found problems with stimulus control and underpowered samples that "commonly produce overestimated effect size estimates." It concludes: "It is premature to offer any bold theoretical statements, definitive empirical pronouncements." — [Elliot 2015, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00368/full)
- The more consistent effects in that review, all with some failed replications, are:
  - Red in achievement contexts increases caution and avoidance and can undermine performance on hard tasks.
  - Red near a female increases attractiveness ratings among heterosexual men.
  - Blue light increases subjective alertness.
  - Source: [Elliot 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00368/full)
- Color-in-context theory (Elliot & Maier 2012) holds that color meaning depends on context and learned associations. Elliot notes the theory is only "vaguely suggestive of precise hypotheses in specific contexts" — [Elliot 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00368/full)
- Labrecque & Milne (2012), "Exciting Red and Competent Blue," *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science*, ran four studies:
  - They mapped hues to brand-personality dimensions.
  - Saturation and value (brightness) amplify personality traits.
  - Color can be used to shift brand personality and purchase intent.
  - Color affects likability and familiarity.
  - Source: [abstract via colab.ws](https://colab.ws/articles/10.1007%2Fs11747-010-0245-y)
- A secondary summary says purchase intent was highest when color matched the brand personality being claimed — [La Fabrique du Net (French)](https://www.lafabriquedunet.fr/agences/tendances/psychologie-des-couleurs-et-identite-de-marque). The full text was not read, so exact hue-trait maps and effect sizes are unverified.

**Older viewers and color:**
- Blue-yellow and pastel discrimination declines with age (see Section 2) — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/02/140220102614.htm); [Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/integrative-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnint.2023.1129315/pdf)

**Feed UI:**
- Facebook added dark mode to its mobile apps and desktop in 2020 — [ExpertBeacon](https://expertbeacon.com/what-is-dark-mode-on-facebook/)
- Dark-mode apps typically use slightly blue-tinted dark grays rather than pure black. Twitter's is about #192734 — [codeburst](https://codeburst.io/50-shades-of-dark-mode-gray-d3e9907b1194)

### Inferences
**Evidence ladder for color choices, strongest first:**
1. Luminance contrast for legibility (strong vision-science evidence).
2. Color that matches the brand or message personality, such as clinical vs natural vs heritage (Labrecque & Milne, moderate).
3. Red as an alarm or danger signal (moderate, context-dependent).
4. Specific hue-emotion claims such as "orange = friendly" or "purple = luxury" (weak; convention or myth).

**Background choices by message (convention plus reasoning):**
- **White / clinical (white, pale blue-gray, navy type).**
  - Suits: "doctor-formulated," "clinically studied," and lab-proof messages.
  - Risks: blends into the light-mode feed (white on white). Add a colored border band, a dark headline bar or a full-bleed photo edge so the ad does not look like a feed post.
- **Dark / cinematic (charcoal, deep navy, black).**
  - Suits: fear, problem-agitation, "the hidden cause," and night or sleep.
  - Strong scroll contrast in light mode. In dark mode it can merge with the UI, so give it a lighter or saturated element along the edges, or a bright product.
  - Keep reversed text to short, large, bold lines (Wheildon/Ogilvy reverse-type caution).
- **Kraft / cream / heritage (off-white, parchment, sepia).**
  - Suits: "old-fashioned remedy," "grandma's recipe," natural or traditional positioning, and advertorial and letter formats.
  - Cream with dark brown or black text gives high contrast and lower glare than pure white. This is consistent with the print guides' off-white matte recommendation, which is my extrapolation to screens.
- **Saturated pop (yellow, orange, red, bright teal).**
  - Suits: offers, urgency, "news," and pattern-interrupt.
  - Stands out in both light and dark mode.
  - For 55+ viewers: no white text on yellow or orange (fails contrast). Use black on yellow, or white on deep red/blue at large sizes.
- **Natural (sage, leaf green, earth tones).**
  - Suits: "plant-based" or "natural" claims. The association is learned, which fits color-in-context theory.
  - Avoid green text on blue, or green next to yellow, for older eyes.

**Emotion-to-color pairings (convention, labeled as such):**

| Emotion / intent | Typical pairing | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Fear / alarm | Red accents on dark | Partly supported by red-avoidance research |
| Anger / injustice ("what they don't tell you") | Black, red, yellow, newspaper-style | Convention |
| Relief / calm | Soft blue, white, plenty of space | Convention |
| Hope / vitality | Warm daylight, greens, golden tones | Convention |
| Trust / authority | Navy, white, and a serif or badge seal | Convention, plus Labrecque & Milne "competent blue" |

**Red and yellow as attention colors.** These work through contrast and learned warning meaning. Use red sparingly for one element (price slash, arrow, "STOP") rather than as the whole background. Elliot's avoidance findings suggest heavy red may make viewers cautious rather than drawn in, which is an untested extrapolation to ads.

**Dark-mode test.** Preview every static on both a white (#FFFFFF) and a near-black dark-gray background. The ad needs a defined edge, such as a border, a contrasting background or a full-bleed image, in both.

### Gaps
- The exact hex values of Facebook and Instagram dark mode (often cited as #18191A / #242526 for Facebook web, and pure black for Instagram) could not be verified in this session.
- I found no controlled study comparing CTR or conversion by ad background color for health products or 55+ audiences.
- Labrecque & Milne's detailed hue-personality results (for example, which hues mapped to sincerity, competence or excitement) were not accessible.
- I found no reliable evidence for claims like "green increases health trust." Treat these as conventions.

## 4. Composition and hierarchy in a 4:5 static: focal point, eye path, scale contrast, white space, placement, grid

### Takeaway
- **Primary visual.** Build around one dominant visual, either the product or a "problem/outcome" image. Pictures capture attention almost regardless of size.
- **Headline.** Make it the largest text, and give text real area: text attention grows with text size.
- **Reading order.** Visual → headline → caption or proof → CTA.
- **Element count.** Keep elements few. Enlarging one element steals attention from the others.

### Cited Findings
**Pieters & Wedel (2004), *Journal of Marketing*, vol. 68:**
- The study eye-tracked 1,363 print ads with more than 3,600 consumers.
- The pictorial element is "superior in capturing attention, independent of its size."
- Attention to text rises with its surface size.
- A 1% larger ad gets about 1% more attention.
- To maximize attention to the whole ad, advertisers are "better off devoting more space to the text portion and increasing the headline size than using large photos."
- Sources: [U-Michigan press release](https://news.umich.edu/print-ad-pictures-less-is-more); [scinapse record](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2147156975)
- **Replication is mixed.** A feature-ad study (Pieters et al. 2007) and a 2010 magazine study found that text size had no significant effect. Enlarging one element reduced attention to the others — [Frontiers in Psychology 2013 review, PMC3856423](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3856423)

**Ogilvy's layout order:**
- Readers' eyes go illustration → headline → caption → copy, so lay out "illustration first... headline second, copy third."
- "Every illustration should have a caption."
- Subheads build readership in long copy.
- Source: [ProfitAdvisors summary](https://www.profitadvisors.com/ogilvy.shtml)
- Ogilvy also said five times as many people read the headline as the body. He opposed "blind" headlines that only make sense with the body copy — [Wikipedia: Confessions of an Advertising Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessions_of_an_Advertising_Man); [Inkbot Design](https://inkbotdesign.com/david-ogilvy/)
- A secondary source says captions are read about twice as much as body text — [search summary of Ogilvy sources](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-david-ogilvy.md) (the exact figure is unverified)

**Clutter:**
- Practitioner rule: "if nothing stands out when they first scan, they'll keep scrolling." Each added block reduces readability — [Alex Cooper, 7 Rules for High Converting Static Ads](https://alexcooper.beehiiv.com/p/7-rules-for-high-converting-static-ads) (practitioner opinion)
- NN/g found older users miss elements "among a profusion of other design elements" — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-for-senior-citizens/)

**Grids:**
- Müller-Brockmann's *Grid Systems in Graphic Design* (1981) frames the grid as cultivating "objectivity instead of subjectivity." Its best-known line is that working with the grid "means submitting to laws of universal validity" — [neugraphic.com](https://neugraphic.com/muller-brockmann/muller-brockmann-text2.html); [CUNY PDF](https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/langecomd3504fa2019/files/2018/10/MullerBrockmann_Grid_Des-Phil.pdf)

### Inferences
**Practical 4:5 (1080x1350) layout system (synthesis):**
- **Margins.** Use a 60–80 px outer margin, with a 12-column or 6-column grid inside it. Keep critical text out of roughly the bottom 10–15% and top ~5%: in the feed the image is followed by the CTA bar, and in some placements by overlaid UI. Check Meta's current safe zones per placement.
- **Three canonical layouts:**
  - **Stacked (Ogilvy-style).** Visual in the top 55–65%, headline beneath, proof line or caption, then CTA or offer bar at the bottom. This mirrors Ogilvy's illustration → headline → copy order and the natural top-down scroll.
  - **Headline-top.** A 2–3 line headline in the top third, the product or hero visual in the middle and lower right third, and a proof badge cluster on the left. Reading order follows a Z path: headline left to right, diagonal to the product, then CTA bottom right.
  - **Split / comparison.** Left vs right, or top vs bottom (before/after, us vs them). Use luminance contrast to separate the halves, not just hue (see Section 2).
- **One focal point.** Make the dominant element 2–3x the visual weight of the next one. Pieters & Wedel's "transfer" finding means every added element dilutes the others.
- **Text-to-image balance.** The pictorial wins the first glance anyway, so give the headline more area than instinct suggests. Do not shrink the headline to enlarge the photo.
- **White space.** For 55+ viewers, leave at least about 20–30% of the canvas as calm, empty or low-detail area around the headline. This is an inference from the "lots of white space" and clutter findings, not a measured number.
- **Product placement.** Put the product in the lower third or along the right-third vertical, on a clean surface. Size it so the label is readable at feed scale if the brand name matters.
- **CTA placement.** Put the CTA at the bottom, centered or right. Meta already provides a CTA button below the image, so the in-image CTA is optional. Use it for offers ("50% off today").
- **Captions.** Captions under a photo (Ogilvy) translate to a short descriptor line under a testimonial photo or a "Dr. ___, MD" credit line.

### Gaps
- No source fetched in this session documents the F-pattern or Z-pattern for single images. The F-pattern comes from NN/g web text-scanning research and transfers only loosely to a single static. Treat Z/F paths as heuristics.
- No source validated the rule of thirds for ads.
- Meta's current placement safe-zone pixel values were not retrieved.

## 5. Classic masters' transferable principles, and "who is the greatest"

### Takeaway
Across the masters, the shared lessons for DR statics are:
- One big, single-minded idea (Lois, Bass, Rand).
- A minimal type palette on a disciplined grid (Vignelli, Müller-Brockmann, Tschichold).
- Ruthless subtraction (Rams).
- Ogilvy's empirically derived readability rules: headline as news or benefit, photo with caption, upper and lower case, serif for long text, avoid long reversed copy.

No authoritative ranking of the "greatest" designer exists. Paul Rand, Saul Bass, Milton Glaser and Massimo Vignelli are the most consistently cited graphic designers, and Ogilvy and Bernbach the most cited ad men.

### Cited Findings
**David Ogilvy:**
- "On the average, five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy"
- He opposed blind headlines.
- "Never set the copy in reverse."
- Use upper and lower case.
- Serif is easiest to read.
- Every illustration gets a caption.
- Subheads, drop-initials (+13% readership) and paragraph spacing (+12%) each help.
- Sources: [Inkbot Design](https://inkbotdesign.com/david-ogilvy/); [ProfitAdvisors](https://www.profitadvisors.com/ogilvy.shtml); [The Weekly Source](https://www.theweeklysource.com.au/lesson-for-self-dont-use-reverse-type-and-capitals-in-advertising)
- Note: the numbered rule lists vary between secondary sources. Check exact wording against *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983).

**Massimo Vignelli:**
- His 1991 SVA exhibition showed work done with only four typefaces: Garamond, Bodoni, Century Expanded and Helvetica.
- In *The Vignelli Canon* he says he can "get along well with a half a dozen, to which I can add another half a dozen, but probably no more." He names Optima, Futura, Univers, Caslon and Baskerville.
- Sources: [Fonts In Use](https://www.fontsinuse.com/uses/14164/massimo-vignelli-s-a-few-basic-typefaces); [UX Collective on the Vignelli Canon](https://uxdesign.cc/the-vignelli-canon-a-design-classic-from-the-last-of-the-modernists-74d6e7dc0169)

**Josef Müller-Brockmann:** He saw grids as cultivating objectivity and as "submitting to laws of universal validity." He is linked to the Swiss Style and to Tschichold's New Typography lineage — [neugraphic.com](https://neugraphic.com/muller-brockmann/muller-brockmann-text2.html); [theartbog review](https://theartbog.com/book-review-grid-systems-in-graphic-design-by-josef-muller-brockmann/)

**George Lois:**
- His "Big Idea" was "crystallizing the unique virtues of a product and searing it into people's minds."
- He made 92 *Esquire* covers (1962–72), which entered MoMA's permanent collection in 2008.
- When asked how to improve the covers, he said they must convey a point of view as forcefully as the magazine's star writers.
- Michael Bierut credits him with founding the "big idea" school of advertising.
- Sources: [NPR Illinois obituary](https://www.nprillinois.org/2022-11-20/george-lois-an-icon-of-ads-and-magazine-covers-has-died-at-91); [Gazette](https://gazettenet.com/?p=263441); [Fonts In Use, Esquire Oct 1966](https://fontsinuse.com/uses/16536/esquire-october-1966)

**Paul Rand, Saul Bass, Milton Glaser** (sources are listicles, with consistent consensus):
- **Rand** created the IBM, UPS and ABC identities and is credited with convincing executives that design is a business asset and with pushing logos toward simplicity.
- **Bass** is known for Hitchcock title sequences, which he essentially invented as an art form, and for logos such as AT&T and United.
- **Glaser** created I ❤ NY, now in MoMA, co-founded *New York* magazine, and was the first graphic designer to receive the National Medal of Arts.
- Other recurring names: Vignelli (NYC subway map), Paula Scher and Otl Aicher (Munich 1972 pictograms).
- Sources: [ManyPixels](https://www.manypixels.co/blog/graphic-design/famous-designers); [NYFA](https://nyfa.edu/student-resources/five-famous-graphic-designers-who-changed-the-industry-forever); [Canva](https://www.canva.com/learn/famous-graphic-designers/)
- No authoritative ranking was found.

### Inferences
**Transfer to DR statics:**
- **Ogilvy → the closest to a DR rulebook:**
  - News or benefit headline, never blind.
  - Photo of the product or person, with a caption.
  - Upper and lower case headline.
  - Avoid reversed long copy; short reversed headlines are acceptable.
  - Use subheads or numbered points for proof. "3 reasons..." and numbered benefit lists are Ogilvy-endorsed.
- **Vignelli / Müller-Brockmann / Tschichold → system discipline:**
  - One or two type families per brand template, in a fixed set of sizes (for example headline / sub / proof / CTA).
  - A fixed margin and column grid reused across every static, so variants test messages rather than layouts.
  - Tschichold's asymmetric "New Typography" supports left-aligned (flush-left, ragged-right) headlines over centered stacks when there are three or more lines. This is my inference; Tschichold's own text was not retrieved.
- **Lois / Bass / Rand → one idea per ad.** Each static should express a single visual metaphor or "big idea" (for example, a joint shown as a rusty hinge) that reads in under 1 second, with the symbol doing the work.
- **Rams ("less but better") → subtraction test.** Remove any element that does not help the viewer understand the claim or act. This aligns with the NN/g clutter findings for older users.
- **"Greatest" summary:**
  - Among graphic designers, Paul Rand is most often named the father of modern corporate identity. Saul Bass is cited for distilling a film or brand into one symbol. Glaser and Vignelli round out most "greatest" lists.
  - In advertising, Ogilvy and Bill Bernbach are most cited, with George Lois for art direction.
  - This is a consensus of popular sources, not a formal ranking.

### Gaps
- Dieter Rams's ten principles of good design and "Weniger, aber besser" ("less but better"), and Tschichold's *Die neue Typographie* (1928) asymmetric layout and sans-serif advocacy, were not verified with a source in this session. They are widely known, but the report writer should cite primary or museum sources (for example Vitsœ's Rams page) before quoting.
- Saul Bass's often-quoted line ("Design is thinking made visual" / "symbolize and summarize") was not verified.
- Milton Glaser's design principles (for example, his "Ten Things I Have Learned" talk) were not retrieved.
- Ogilvy's "photos beat drawings" and "before/after" claims were not confirmed in the sources fetched. They are commonly attributed to *Ogilvy on Advertising* but need a primary citation.
- No authoritative ranking (AIGA, D&AD, academic survey) of "greatest designer" was found.
